\begin{abstract}
Large language models (LLMs) have shown competence in abstract mathematical reasoning.
Some frontier models have even been able to solve open problems in combinatorics.
However, these models often fail to consistently produce correct, verifiable results, and require
much human intervention and iteration.
Furthermore, LLMs struggle in the niche domain of quantum computing, where the potential training material is
limited compared to a more general and well-studied field such as combinatorics.
We propose an agentic framework for abstract quantum computing algorithm design,
where core definitions and axioms of the field are defined in Lean, goals can be formally specified,
and correctness is checked at every step with the Lean proof-checker.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Quantum computing (QC) has the potential to exponentially speed up
many classical computing algorithms \cite{brandao2010exponential}.
However, solutions to quantum computing problems are often hard to verify,
given that quantum programs cannot be efficiently simulated on a classical computer,
and access to currently-available quantum machines is very expensive.
As a result, many programs can only be verified analytically.
This presents as particular challenge for LLM agents.

So far, most attempts to use LLMs for quantum circuit synthesis have
used Qiskit or OpenQASM as the underlying environment; circuits are
written as code in these languages, then executed on a quantum simulator,
and the results are compared with the expected answer.
We propose a different approach: quantum computing foundamentals can be formalized in a
proof assistant language such as Lean, then goals such as circuit synthesis
can be posed formally. An LLM can then be instructed to not only provide code to construct
a circuit, but also a proof-of-correctness, allowing for automatic verification
without the need for expensive numerical simulation.
Once verified, abstract circuit definitions can then be compiled to OpenQASM or Qiskit
circuits and run on quantum machines.

\section{Proposed Approach}

\paragraph{Quantum Formalization}

Formalization of quantum computing in proof assistant languages has already been
extensively explored. \textbf{SQIR} \cite{hietala2021verified} introduces a formalization
of quantum circuits using density matrices, \textbf{VyZX} \cite{lehmann2026vy}
formalizes the ZX-calculus, a graphical language for reasoning about quantum circuits (both are written in Coq).
This allows for machine-checked proofs of statements about circuits,
where results can be proved for \textit{all} possible inputs rather than just a single instance.
For example, Figure~\ref{fig:ghz} shows the GHZ state preparation task as an agent would encounter it:
a formal specification, an implementation, and the resulting circuit.

\paragraph{Theorem Proving}

Much research has been done on automating Lean theorem proving with artificial intelligence.
\textbf{AlphaProof} \cite{hubert2025olympiad} uses end-to-end reinforcement learning to train a
neural network for solving math olympiad problems, and achieves significant success.
\textbf{APOLLO} \cite{ospanov2026apollo} uses LLMs to generate proofs in Lean for formal math benchmarks,
using a feedback-loop system where the proof state and compiler errors are fed back into the LLM context,
and new sub-goals are extracted from partially correct proofs.
Our approach would extend these frameworks to optimize them specifically for quantum computing,
with a formalized QC language similar to \textbf{SQIR} or \textbf{VyZX} as the underlying
``engine,'' allowing for humans to specify formal goals, and LLMs to generate verified solutions.

\paragraph{Agentic Engineering}

The rapid uptake of auto-coding frameworks such as Codex or Claude Code in recent months demonstrates
the benefits of ``agentic'' LLMs. The difference between agentic and regular LLMs
is not in the models themselves, but rather in the structure they are placed into, and the tools
they are given \cite{plaat2025agentic}.
For example, agentic coding involves placing an LLM into a real coding environment, where the model
can write code, run Bash commands, or search the web.
Our approach towards quantum algorithm design would include a tailored agentic environment,
which would be made up of specialized agents for tasks such as paper reading, plan generation,
circuit synthesis, circuit verification, or code refactoring.
Each agent would have their own prompt, permissions, and tools.
For example, an agent responsible for reading papers may use a tool for searching the arxiv.org database,
and vision tools for reading figures and plots, but would not have permission to write and run code.

\paragraph{Reinforcement Learning}

LLMs are unlikely to have the capabilities required for QC research ``out of the box.''
Even on basic theorem proving tasks, frontier models often require hundreds or even thousands of iterations
before converging on the correct solution. For this reason, researchers have recently begun
to train LLMs on large datasets of mathematical problems, creating an ``expert'' on
the task of mathematical theorem proving \cite{lin2025goedel,xin2024deepseek}.
These models can often achieve superior performance to frontier models, despite being
much smaller and requiring much fewer resources to run.
\textbf{AlphaProof} even uses test-time reinforcement learning to learn ``on the fly''
when solving a specific problem by generating many similar problems to train on.
We plan to carry this approach over to quantum computing.
For example, we can generate random circuit identities
to either prove or disprove, or random circuits using basis gates as well as building blocks such as
loops and recursion, and random circuit properties to either prove or disprove.
This would allow us to build a set of training data without \textit{any} human input,
whereas \textbf{AlphaProof} and others require at least some human-written mathematical text and code.

\section{Benchmarking}

In order to evaluate the performance of our system, we must build a set of benchmark
tasks for the system to complete. We propose several options for the source of these tasks,
and discuss the potential strengths and downsides of each option.

\paragraph{Existing Results}

One potential source of benchmark tasks is existing results.
These could come from textbooks or published papers, but would require
human formalization of each goal.
There is also the potential for unwanted information about the solution
to leak into the LLM context, or to have been included in the training data,
effectively allowing the model to ``cheat.''
This could be avoided by using results which are unpublished, but there
are unlikely to be enough unpublished quantum computing results that could
be collated from different researchers in order to build a representative benchmark.

\paragraph{Synthetic Data}

It is also possible to generate benchmarking data synthetically.
The process that \textbf{AlphaProof} uses to generate synthetic goals for training
could also be used to generate a large set of benchmark problems.
However, this approach depends on the novelty of the generated goals.
A sophisticated system may have to be developed to ensure novelty and representativeness,
to avoid ending up with a large set of almost-identical
or already-existing goals.

\paragraph{Open Problems}

Solving a non-trivial open problem within a field is possibly the best way to
show the performance of an agentic research system.
A set of open problems could be compiled and formalized into Lean,
then passed to the LLM to solve.
In the field of combinatorics, this has been done with the ``Erdős Problems.''
The problems are a set of over 1000 similar combinatorics problems compiled by
the mathematician Paul Erdős over his lifetime.
Only about half of the problems have known solutions, resulting in an excellent
benchmark for AI; a model can be tested both on solved and unsolved problems,
and ability to recreate human solutions as well as generate new solutions is evaluated.
A similar benchmark could be compiled for quantum computing, combining both solved
and unsolved problems in the field.
