\begin{abstract}
Large language models (LLMs) have shown competence in abstract mathematical research and theorem proving.
Some frontier models have even been able to solve non-trivial open problems in combinatorics.
However, these models often fail to consistently produce correct, verifiable results, and require
much human intervention and iteration.
Furthermore, LLMs struggle in the niche domain of quantum computing, where the potential training material is
limited compared to a more general and well-studied field such as combinatorics.
We present a work-in-progress agentic framework for abstract quantum computing algorithm design.
Core definitions and axioms of the field are human-defined in Lean, as are goals,
and correctness is checked at every step with the Lean proof-checker.
\end{abstract}




\section{Introduction}

Quantum computing has the potential to exponentially speed up
many classical computing algorithms \cite{brandao2010exponential}.
However, solutions to quantum computing problems are often hard to verify,
given that quantum programs cannot be efficiently simulated on a classical computer [CITE],
and access to currently-available quantum machines is very expensive.
Because of this, correctness of quantum algorithms can often only be proved analytically.
For these reasons, quantum algorithm design
presents a particular challenge for LLM agents, and artificial intelligence agents more broadly,
given that their training loops rely on tasks for which solutions can be easily verified or scored.

Previous applications of artificial intelligence (AI) to quantum computing have fallen
into two categories. The first is numerical optimization algorithms, which use neural
nets and reinforcement learning to optimize low-level aspects of quantum algorithm
design, such as direct unitary synthesis, circuit optimization, or error correcting
\cite{zhang2020topological,alam2023quantum,bao2024twisty}.
This area has shown some promise, but these methods do not scale to large numbers of qubits,
making them impractical for large-scale synthesis tasks.
The second, more recent category, is the use of LLMs to perform quantum computing tasks.
Several works have used LLMs to write Qiskit or OpenQASM code directly, which is then
run through a local simulator to produce results [CITE].
This suffers from the same problem of the first approach; it does not generalize beyond
small numbers of qubits, and code is only verified on the examples which it is tested
against.

In order to solve these problems, we build a domain-specific benchmark of
abstract quantum computing tasks, formalized in Lean.
The tasks range from proving simple lemmas about matrices, to more complex theorems
about circuit synthesis, to implementing circuit design specifications.
We test several state-of-the art LLMs on these problems, using the coding harness
OpenCode \cite{opencode}, equipped with specialized Lean tools.
We use the Lean tool `Comparator' [CITE] to run a strict verification of the LLM-generated code,
and run the entire setup inside of a containerized environment to ensure reproducibility.

\paragraph{Qubit States}

We can define a Hilbert space in Lean using the existing \verb|EuclideanSpace| type,
which gives access to the inner product required to satisfy the rules of a Hilbert space.
An $n$-qubit Hilbert space is then defined as a $2^n$-dimensional complex Euclidian space, and
an $n$-qubit state is defined as an element of the $n$-qubit Hilbert space,
provided that the norm of the element is 1.
The basis states $|0\rangle$ and $|1\rangle$ are then defined as the basis vectors
of the 2-dimensional complex Euclidean space.
